# Distributed Systems
Why Do Distributed Systems Need Consensus Protocols? | Advanced | Consensus | Consensus lets a set of nodes agree on one ordered log of decisions even when some nodes fail or messages are delayed. | It underpins replicated databases, cluster metadata, and leader election in reliable systems. | What assumptions do consensus protocols make about failures and communication? | consensus, replication, fault-tolerance
Why Is Exactly-Once Delivery So Difficult? | Advanced | Fault Tolerance | A sender cannot distinguish a lost message from a lost acknowledgment. Without durable deduplication on the receiver, retries inevitably duplicate effects. | Many systems promise exactly-once while actually delivering at-least-once with idempotent consumption. | Where does the deduplication state have to live for exactly-once effects to be real? | exactly-once, messaging, retries
How Does a Cluster Detect a Failed Node? | Intermediate | Fault Tolerance | Nodes use heartbeats, timeouts, and indirect probes to infer failure, but evidence is always delayed and ambiguous. Failure detection is a suspicion, not a fact. | Every failover decision depends on a detector that can be wrong in both directions. | Why can a slow node be mistaken for a dead one, and what does that trigger? | failure-detection, heartbeats, clusters
What Does the CAP Theorem Actually Describe? | Intermediate | Replication | CAP states that during a network partition a system cannot provide both linearizable consistency and availability. It is about behavior under partition, not a permanent three-way choice. | It is widely quoted and frequently misapplied to ordinary latency variations. | What does a system have to give up during a partition, and what can it preserve? | cap-theorem, consistency, availability
How Do Distributed Systems Handle Clock Skew? | Advanced | Ordering | Physical clocks drift and cannot provide a reliable global order. Systems use logical clocks, hybrid clocks, or bounded-drift assumptions to reason about event order. | Timestamps that look authoritative can silently reorder events across machines. | Why are last-write-wins conflicts dangerous when clocks disagree? | clock-skew, ordering, causality
Why Are Distributed Transactions Expensive? | Advanced | Distributed Storage | Coordinating a commit across multiple nodes requires extra rounds, durable state, and blocking during failures, which limits throughput and availability. | The cost is the reason many architectures choose sagas or eventual consistency instead. | What does two-phase commit do when the coordinator fails after participants prepare? | transactions, 2pc, coordination
What Happens When a Consensus Leader Is Partitioned? | Advanced | Consensus | A leader isolated from the majority cannot commit new entries, while a newly elected leader can. When the partition heals, the old leader must reconcile its log. | It tests whether your mental model of leader election survives the messy failure case. | How does a consensus protocol prevent the old leader from serving stale writes after healing? | raft, leader-election, partitions
How Do Vector Clocks Track Causality? | Advanced | Ordering | Vector clocks attach per-node counters to events so a reader can tell whether one event happened before another or the two are concurrent. | They make causality visible without relying on synchronized wall clocks. | Why are vector clocks impractical for very large systems? | vector-clocks, causality, distributed
Why Do Distributed Queues Deliver Messages More Than Once? | Intermediate | Messaging | Acknowledgments can be lost after the consumer processes a message but before the broker records it, so the broker must redeliver. | Consumer code must be idempotent because duplicate delivery is a property of the protocol, not a bug. | What state would a consumer need to keep to safely discard a duplicate message? | message-queues, at-least-once, idempotency
Why Do Distributed Systems Need Replicas? | Beginner | Replication | Replicas keep additional copies of data or services so the system can survive machine failures and serve reads from more locations. | Replication is the most common answer to both availability and read scaling, with consistency costs attached. | What has to happen for a write to be considered durable across replicas? | replication, availability, fault-tolerance
