# Computer Science Theory
What Makes a Problem NP-Complete? | Advanced | Complexity Theory | NP-complete problems are in NP and at least as hard as every problem in NP under polynomial reductions. A fast exact solution to one would solve them all. | Recognizing NP-completeness stops teams from searching for an efficient exact algorithm that probably does not exist. | How would you prove a new scheduling problem is NP-complete? | np-complete, complexity, reductions
Why Can No Algorithm Solve the Halting Problem? | Intermediate | Computability | A general algorithm cannot decide whether every program halts, because a diagonal argument constructs a program whose behavior contradicts any supposed decider. | It marks a boundary on what static analysis and automation can guarantee. | What happens when you run a supposed halting decider on a program built to contradict it? | halting-problem, computability, undecidability
How Does Big-O Hide Important Constants? | Beginner | Complexity Theory | Asymptotic notation ignores constant factors and lower-order terms that can dominate on realistic input sizes. Two O(n log n) algorithms may differ by an order of magnitude. | Algorithm choice needs measurements at the sizes you actually run. | At what input size does an asymptotically slower algorithm become preferable? | big-o, constants, performance
What Is the Difference Between P and NP? | Intermediate | Complexity Theory | P contains problems solvable quickly; NP contains problems whose solutions can be verified quickly. Whether P equals NP is unknown, with enormous practical stakes. | It frames why some problems resist efficient exact solution. | Why does verifying a solution not imply being able to find one efficiently? | p-vs-np, complexity, verification
How Does Information Theory Bound Compression? | Advanced | Information Theory | Entropy measures the average information content of a source, setting a lower bound on how much lossless compression is possible for that distribution. | It explains why some data compresses well and some cannot be compressed further. | Why can a truly random file not be shortened by a general-purpose compressor? | information-theory, entropy, compression
What Makes a Problem Approximation-Hard? | Advanced | Complexity Theory | Some optimization problems cannot be approximated within certain ratios unless major complexity classes collapse, so heuristics lack worst-case guarantees. | Knowing approximation hardness prevents overpromising on algorithm quality. | Why does hardness of approximation require a gap-preserving reduction? | approximation, hardness, optimization
Why Is the Turing Machine Still a Useful Model? | Beginner | Automata | The Turing machine provides a simple, precise definition of effective computation that underpins decidability, complexity, and language theory. | It abstracts away hardware while preserving what matters for fundamental limits. | Why does the Church-Turing thesis make the model more than a historical artifact? | turing-machine, models, computability
How Do Lower Bounds Prove an Algorithm Is Optimal? | Advanced | Complexity Theory | A lower bound shows that no algorithm in a model of computation can do better than a stated cost, often using adversary or decision-tree arguments. | Without a lower bound, an algorithm may be improved; with one, effort should go elsewhere. | How does an adversary argument establish a comparison-sorting lower bound? | lower-bounds, optimality, complexity
What Is the Difference Between Decidability and Complexity? | Advanced | Complexity Theory | Decidability asks whether a problem can be solved at all; complexity asks how much time or space is required. A problem can be decidable yet intractable. | The distinction matters when choosing between exact algorithms, approximations, and giving up. | Why is a decidable problem not automatically practical? | decidability, tractability, complexity
