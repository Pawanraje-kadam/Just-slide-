# Cryptography & Applied Security
What Does a Digital Signature Actually Prove? | Intermediate | Signatures | A valid signature shows that the holder of a private key approved specific bytes. It does not prove intent, identity, or that the signed content is safe. | Signature verification is only as meaningful as the key it trusts and the bytes it covers. | What exactly gets hashed and signed in a typical message-signing scheme? | digital-signatures, hashing, trust
Why Is Rolling Your Own Crypto So Dangerous? | Beginner | Applied Crypto | Cryptographic code fails silently: a small deviation from the specification can preserve functionality while destroying security. Side channels and misuse make it worse. | The hardest part is not encryption; it is getting every detail and protocol interaction right. | What could go wrong if you reuse a nonce in a stream cipher? | crypto-misuse, encryption, security
How Do Password Hashing Functions Resist Brute Force? | Beginner | Key Management | Password hashes are deliberately slow and memory-hard, with per-user salts, so attackers cannot cheaply precompute or parallelize guesses across stolen databases. | Password storage decisions determine how damaging a database breach becomes. | Why is a fast general-purpose hash unsuitable for passwords? | password-hashing, salting, breach
What Makes Key Rotation Hard? | Advanced | Key Management | Rotating keys requires old keys for decryption, new keys for encryption, versioned ciphertext, and coordinated rollout across services without downtime. | Rotation is a core control that fails when key versions are not explicit in data. | How would you rotate a key used to encrypt millions of stored records? | key-rotation, key-management, operations
How Does Forward Secrecy Limit the Damage of a Leaked Key? | Advanced | Secure Protocols | Forward secrecy derives per-session keys from ephemeral exchanges, so compromising a long-term key later does not decrypt recorded traffic. | It changes the consequences of a key compromise from retrospective to limited. | Why does an ephemeral Diffie-Hellman exchange protect past sessions? | forward-secrecy, tls, key-exchange
Why Is Randomness a Security Requirement? | Intermediate | Applied Crypto | Keys, nonces, and salts must be unpredictable to attackers. Reused or predictable values can collapse the security of an otherwise correct construction. | Randomness failures have broken real-world systems without any flaw in the cipher itself. | What is the difference between a general-purpose random number and a cryptographic one? | randomness, nonces, crypto-failures
What Is the Difference Between Encryption and Hashing? | Beginner | Applied Crypto | Encryption is reversible with a key; hashing is a one-way transformation. Confusing the two leads to storing recoverable secrets where verification is enough. | The distinction decides whether a compromised database reveals plaintext. | Why is hashing appropriate for password verification but not for storing messages? | encryption, hashing, fundamentals
How Do Zero-Knowledge Proofs Work? | Advanced | Applied Crypto | A zero-knowledge proof convinces a verifier that a statement is true without revealing the witness. Interactive challenges and Fiat-Shamir transforms make this practical. | The idea enables privacy-preserving verification in identity and blockchain systems. | What does the verifier learn from a proof besides the truth of the statement? | zero-knowledge, proofs, privacy
Why Do Cryptographic Protocols Fail Even When Ciphers Work? | Advanced | Secure Protocols | Real protocols must handle authentication, replay, ordering, downgrade, and key freshness. Individual primitives can be secure while the composition is not. | Most practical crypto failures are protocol design failures, not broken algorithms. | What replay protection does a protocol need beyond encryption? | protocol-design, replay-attacks, crypto
